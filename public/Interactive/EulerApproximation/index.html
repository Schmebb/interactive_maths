Differential equations — numerical methods

Euler's tangent walk

The point of Euler's method is to approximate the numerical solution to a differential equation when an exact formula cannot be found. Real life is messy, and we need ways to find solutions to rates of change.

Why it drifts, and what to do

The error is roughly proportional to h

Each step throws away the curvature — you walked a straight line where the real solution bends. Those small mistakes accumulate, and the accumulated error shrinks in step with h: halve the step size and you roughly halve the gap at the end.

Press Halve h a few times and watch the error readout. It falls by about half each press, while the number of steps doubles. That trade — accuracy against work — is the whole subject in miniature.

Where it gives up

Euler is honest but crude. On a solution that curves hard, or one that blows up (try Runaway), the walk wanders off long before the true curve does. Very small steps help, until rounding error starts to bite.

The fixes all use the same trick: sample the slope more than once per step before committing. Averaging the slope at both ends gives Heun's method; four samples per step gives Runge–Kutta 4, which draws the green reference curve on this page.

The whole method, three moves

It really is this short. Everything else is bookkeeping.

  1. 1 — Read the slope

    You are standing at (xn, yn). The differential equation tells you the slope there: f(xn, yn).

  2. 2 — Step along it

    Move h to the right and rise by h·f(xn, yn), pretending the curve is straight for that short stretch.

  3. 3 — Re-aim, repeat

    You have landed somewhere new. Read the slope again from there and take the next step. The curve is rebuilt one tangent at a time.

xn+1 = xn + h
yn+1 = yn + h*f(xn, yn)

Starting from a known point (x0, y0) — the initial condition. Without one there is nowhere to stand.

A differential equation hands you a rule for the slope at every point — but not the curve itself. Most of the equations that describe real systems (cooling coffee, spreading disease, a falling body with drag) have no tidy formula you can solve for by hand.

So you stop looking for the formula. You stand on the one point you know, aim along the tangent, take a short step of width h, and re-aim. Repeat. The kinked path you leave behind is the approximation.
